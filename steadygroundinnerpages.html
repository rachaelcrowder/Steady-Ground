Page — The Programme

The Programme

Two days. One cohort.
Lasting practice.

Steady Ground is a fully co-facilitated, two-day train-the-trainer programme for gender-based violence educators. It equips facilitators with the trauma-informed skills, theoretical grounding, and practical tools to design and deliver TFGBV educational sessions safely, ethically, and effectively.

Format & Cohort

Designed for how adult learning actually works.

Steady Ground is delivered as two three-hour in-person sessions, scheduled on consecutive or closely spaced mornings. Each session combines didactic teaching, live modelling, supervised practice, case-based learning, and structured debrief.

Recommended cohort size is 16–24 participants. Even numbers are preferred to support dyad and triad work throughout both days. Participants complete a short pre-work assignment (approximately 35 minutes of video) before Day 1.

Four to six weeks after Day 2, the cohort reconvenes for a 90-minute Community of Practice session — a structured, co-facilitated follow-up that bridges the gap between initial training and embedded practice change.

"Co-facilitation is not a contingency. It is pedagogy."

Steady Ground Design Principles

Steady Ground is fully co-facilitated throughout both days. Two practitioners sharing active teaching responsibility models the co-regulation, shared power, and mutual accountability that participants are being trained to develop. Participants don't just hear about these principles — they experience them in the room.

The second facilitator leads content segments, runs role-play rounds, tracks group regulation, and accompanies a participant to the quiet room when needed. This is a full teaching partnership, not a support role.

Day 1 · Foundations

The principles, the neuroscience, the protocol.

The first day builds the theoretical and relational foundation participants will draw on for the rest of their facilitation lives. By the end of Day 1, participants have moved from intellectual understanding to embodied experience of the core concepts.

Opening

Welcome, community agreements, and nervous system orientation

The day opens with a brief grounding practice, community agreement-building, and an orientation to how the training itself is designed according to the principles it teaches. The structure of the day is made visible and transparent from the start.

Module 1

Trauma-Informed Principles: SAMHSA in Practice

An introduction to SAMHSA's six trauma-informed principles — safety, trustworthiness, peer support, collaboration, empowerment, and cultural humility — applied not to service delivery but to the design of learning environments where participants may carry lived experience of the content being discussed. Participants examine what each principle looks like in a workshop room.

Module 2

The TFGBV Landscape: Evidence-not-Exposure

A grounded overview of the nature, prevalence, and impact of tech-facilitated gender-based violence — taught through categories, research evidence, and survivor-informed framing. No abusive content is displayed, read aloud, or reenacted. This posture is modelled throughout and is central to what participants are being trained to do.

Module 3

Polyvagal Basics and the Co-Regulation Lab

An accessible introduction to Polyvagal Theory — the three autonomic circuits governing human response, and what they mean for a room full of people engaging with difficult content. Participants complete a structured co-regulation exercise that produces a genuine, first-person experience of the concepts rather than an intellectual understanding of them.

Module 4

Parts-Informed Facilitation

An introduction to Internal Family Systems as a facilitation lens. Participants learn to recognize protective responses in groups — dominance, over-helping, checking out, humor-deflection — as adaptive rather than problematic, and to meet them with curiosity rather than judgment.

Module 5

Micro-Skills, Invitational Language, and the CARE Protocol

Participants learn the concrete language of trauma-informed facilitation: how to open a session, issue content notes, offer genuine choice, and slow their pacing. The CARE Protocol (Center, Acknowledge, Restore Choice, Enlist Support) is introduced as a four-step framework for facilitating disclosures, strong affect, and common facilitation ruptures. Case studies are used to practice application.

Day 2 · Application & Practice

Rupture, repair, and the skills that hold.

The second day moves from knowledge to practiced competence. Participants apply what they have learned in structured role-play scenarios, deepen their ethical grounding, and leave with a personal care plan and a take-home design exercise.

Opening

Check-in and Day 1 integration

A brief grounding practice and reflective check-in to surface what has settled since Day 1, what questions have emerged, and what participants most want to practice today.

Module 6

Rupture and Repair in Context

What does it mean for something to go wrong in the room — and what does it mean to repair it? Participants examine common facilitation ruptures specific to TFGBV content: disclosures, victim-blaming, tone-policing, microaggressions, flooding, and live threat notifications. The difference between rupture and failure is explored, and the possibility of repair becomes part of participants' facilitation vocabulary.

Practice · Round 1

Role-Play with Competency Feedback

Structured role-play in small groups using facilitated scenario cards. Each participant has a chance to practice a facilitation rupture response while their partner plays the observer role using a structured competency feedback tool. Debrief is facilitated by the training team.

Practice · Round 2

Advanced Scenarios

A second round of role-play using more complex scenarios: a live threat notification from a participant, a participant experiencing flooding, a microaggression in the room. Participants choose their scenario based on what they most need to practice.

Module 7

Ethics, Scope, and PEI Referral Pathways

Facilitators are not counsellors, and the training is clear about scope. This module addresses confidentiality limits, mandatory reporting obligations, and how to navigate disclosures that exceed a facilitator's scope. Participants leave with a PEI referral map of eight local organizations with contact information and warm handoff language.

Module 8

Trauma-Informed Presentation Design (introduced)

An introduction to a six-section arc for designing trauma-informed presentations on online harassment. This is introduced in the session and completed as a take-home design exercise, using a structured slide map template. The goal is for each participant to leave with a draft design adapted to their specific audience.

Closing

Facilitator Sustainability and Personal Care Planning

The session closes with an honest conversation about the cost of this work — vicarious trauma, moral distress, and the particular demands of teaching traumatogenic content — and participants complete a personal facilitator care plan. The Community of Practice session and website resources are introduced. The training closes the way it opened: together, grounded, and with care.

Learning Outcomes

What participants are able to do when they leave.

By the close of Day 2, participants will be able to:

Explain SAMHSA's six trauma-informed principles and apply them to their own facilitation design
Use Polyvagal Theory basics: co-regulation, pacing, and creating neurological safety in a workshop setting
Recognize activation and protective patterns in groups without pathologizing, and respond with appropriate tools
Apply the CARE Protocol to common facilitation ruptures including disclosure, strong affect, microaggressions, victim-blaming, and tone-policing
Facilitate TFGBV content using an evidence-not-exposure posture, content notes, and invitational language
Navigate scope, confidentiality limits, and PEI referral pathways ethically
Design a trauma-informed presentation on online harassment adapted to their specific audience
Name their own vicarious trauma risks and commit to a personal facilitator care plan

Page — The Framework

The Framework

Grounded in evidence,
built for practice.

Steady Ground integrates four evidence-based frameworks into a coherent, field-tested facilitation practice. Each one shapes not only what participants learn but the learning environment they experience.

Why four frameworks?

Most facilitation training teaches content. This one teaches practice.

Knowing what TFGBV is does not prepare a facilitator for the moment someone in the room is living it. Understanding trauma-informed principles intellectually is not the same as holding a room according to them. The frameworks at the heart of Steady Ground are integrated and mutually reinforcing — chosen not because they are individually persuasive, but because together they describe the full terrain of what happens in a room where difficult content is being taught.

SAMHSA establishes the principles. Polyvagal Theory explains the neuroscience. IFS offers the relational lens. Feminist pedagogy holds the structural analysis. Each one is essential; none of them is sufficient alone.

"The form of a learning experience teaches as much as its content."

Intersectional Feminist Pedagogy principle

Steady Ground is not a programme about these frameworks. It is a programme that enacts them. The learning environment is designed according to the same principles it teaches — which means participants don't just hear about co-regulation, shared power, and genuine choice. They experience them, in the room, with their training cohort.

Framework 1 · SAMHSA

The six trauma-informed principles — in the room, not just in theory.

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) framework articulates six core principles for trauma-informed approaches. In Steady Ground, these principles are applied not to survivor-facing service delivery, but to the design of any learning environment where participants may carry lived experience of the content being discussed.

1

Safety

Physical and emotional safety is established — not assumed — through transparent communication, consistent structure, community agreements, and the provision of genuine choices about participation. A quiet room is available throughout both days. Participants always know what is coming next.

2

Trustworthiness & Transparency

The programme's design, purpose, and structure are made explicit. Facilitators explain why they do what they do. Scope and confidentiality limits are clearly stated. Participants are not surprised by anything about the day, including difficult content.

3

Peer Support

The cohort model is central to Steady Ground's design. Participants learn alongside people doing the same work, and peer consultation is structured into both days and the follow-up Community of Practice session. This work is not done alone.

4

Collaboration & Mutuality

The power dynamic between facilitators and participants is made visible and actively flattened. Community agreements are built together, not handed down. Participants shape the session through their expertise and experience.

5

Empowerment, Voice & Choice

Participation is always invitational. Content notes are offered before difficult material. Participants are given genuine options about how they engage — and the options are real, not performative. The programme models the exact facilitation behaviour it is teaching.

6

Cultural, Historical & Gender Issues

TFGBV is produced by systems of power, not individuals. The programme's analysis is intersectional throughout, attending explicitly to how race, disability, Indigeneity, and sexual orientation compound vulnerability to online harassment and shape what safety looks like in a room.

Framework 2 · Polyvagal Theory

The neuroscience of why safety is not just a feeling.

Porges' Polyvagal Theory describes three autonomic circuits governing human response to perceived safety and threat. The ventral vagal complex supports social engagement, connection, and learning. The sympathetic system drives fight and flight. The dorsal vagal complex governs freeze and collapse. These states are not chosen — they are neurological responses that happen to us, in real time, in response to what the body perceives as safe or dangerous.

For facilitators delivering TFGBV education, this matters at every level. Participants who carry lived experience of the content may enter a sympathetic or dorsal state without any visible warning sign. A facilitator who cannot recognize the difference between disengagement and freeze, or between strong engagement and activation, will respond in ways that escalate rather than resolve the situation.

Co-regulation as a facilitation tool

Co-regulation is the process by which one regulated nervous system helps regulate another. When a facilitator is calm, their voice, pace, posture, and presence have a measurable effect on the nervous systems around them. This is not metaphor — it is neurobiological reality, and it is the primary reason that facilitator self-regulation is a professional competency, not a personal preference.

Steady Ground trains facilitators to recognize activation cues in groups, to slow their own pace and voice when the room's regulation is shifting, and to use the presence of a co-facilitator — the most powerful co-regulatory resource in a co-facilitated room — deliberately.

Research basis

Learning requires a regulated nervous system

When a learning environment feels unpredictable or emotionally charged, stress disrupts prefrontal cortex function. Attention narrows. Memory encoding is compromised. This is not about willingness to learn — it is neurobiology. The design of a predictable, structured, and physically comfortable learning environment is an evidence-based imperative.

In the room

What this looks like in practice

Facilitators learn to read autonomic cues — scanning for signs of activation or shutdown — and to respond before rupture, not after. The co-regulation lab in Day 1 produces a first-person experience of these dynamics rather than an intellectual understanding of them.

Framework 3 · Internal Family Systems

Protective patterns — with curiosity, not judgment.

Internal Family Systems (IFS), developed by Richard Schwartz, is a non-pathologizing framework for understanding protective responses in groups. In any learning environment where content is emotionally charged, participants may present with "parts" that have developed to manage vulnerability: the participant who dominates discussion, the one who deflects with humour, the one who becomes very still and checks out, the one who rushes to fix everyone else's distress.

These responses are not problems to be managed. They are adaptive protections — the result of prior experiences in rooms where it was not safe to be openly affected. A parts-informed facilitator recognizes them as such, and responds with curiosity and genuine warmth rather than redirection, correction, or performance of neutrality.

What parts-informed facilitation looks like

It means not calling on the person who has checked out, and not filling the space they have left with your own material. It means acknowledging the protector who is dominating — directly, warmly, and without shaming — and creating enough space for others to contribute. It means not being alarmed by the person in tears, and not being deceived by the person laughing.

IFS offers facilitators a vocabulary for internal experience that is both accessible and clinically grounded, and that works as well in a training room as in a therapy room.

"Protective responses in groups are adaptive — they tell you something about the room."

Steady Ground, Day 1
Key distinction

Parts vs. pathology

A parts-informed lens is a non-pathologizing one. The participant who is deflecting with humour is not being difficult. The participant who has gone very quiet is not being passive-aggressive. Each is doing exactly what they know how to do in a room that feels like it might not be safe. The facilitator's job is to make the room safer — not to manage the symptom.

Framework 4 · Feminist Pedagogy

The structure of a room is also a message about power.

Intersectional feminist pedagogy holds that GBV is produced by systems of power, not by individual perpetrators. This analysis is not background knowledge — it is the lens through which every element of the training is designed and delivered.

What does it mean, practically? It means the training room is designed to enact shared power, not to describe it. Community agreements are built together. Facilitator expertise is presented as one kind of knowing, not the only kind. The lived experience participants bring to the room is treated as expertise, not as risk to be managed.

Gender-transformative, not gender-accommodating

There is an important distinction between training that accommodates gender — acknowledging that women face specific challenges and trying not to make things worse — and training that is gender-transformative, meaning it actively challenges the power structures and norms that produce gender inequality. Steady Ground is the latter. It attends explicitly to how gender compounds vulnerability to TFGBV, and it treats the elimination of that vulnerability as a structural, not an individual, project.

Intersectionality as method

Race, disability, Indigeneity, gender expression, and sexual orientation are not add-ons to the gender analysis. They are part of it. Women facing compounded marginalization on the basis of these identities experience heightened rates and more severe forms of TFGBV. The training content and facilitation approach must reflect this complexity — not in a perfunctory way, but as a fundamental orientation to the work.

Design principle

The form teaches as much as the content

A training about shared power that does not share power has already undermined its own message. Steady Ground is designed so that the experience of being in the room confirms and extends what the content teaches — not because it is tidy, but because it is true.

Evidence-not-exposure

Teaching TFGBV without causing it

A feminist approach to TFGBV education means not reproducing harm in the name of education. Abusive content is never displayed, read aloud, or re-enacted. Facilitators teach through evidence, categories, and survivor-informed framing — and they are trained to facilitate the same way.

Ongoing Learning

The training doesn't end when the room empties.

The Community of Practice session is the evidence-based bridge between what participants learn in the room and what they actually do in their facilitation practice. It is also where the real learning begins.

Community of Practice

Because practice change happens in between the sessions.

The research on train-the-trainer models is consistent on this point: the effectiveness of a training is not determined in the room. It is determined in the weeks that follow — when participants return to their organizations and try to apply what they learned without the training team in the room, without the cohort around them, and often without any structured support for the challenges they encounter.

The Steady Ground Community of Practice session addresses this directly. Held four to six weeks after Day 2, it is a 90-minute, co-facilitated session that gives participants a structured space to debrief their real facilitation experiences, consult with peers and facilitators, and renew their commitments to practice.

The CoP session is not a review of training content. It is a peer consultation that uses training frameworks to work through real situations participants have encountered in the field — and that begins from the assumption that each participant has already done something hard, and deserves support in making sense of it.

Format

90 minutes · co-facilitated · in-person or virtual

The CoP session can be held in person or virtually, depending on the cohort's needs. It is structured as peer consultation: participants bring situations they have encountered since the training and work through them using the frameworks and tools from both training days.

Structure

What happens in the room

The session opens with a brief check-in, moves through structured peer consultation on facilitation situations participants bring, includes a facilitation debrief for any completed presentations, reviews and renews personal care commitments, and closes with a design review for upcoming facilitation work. Resources added to the hub since training are introduced.

What the Evidence Says

Why follow-up makes the difference.

A 2023 study published in the Journal of Clinical Psychiatry found that trauma-informed training resulted in significant, sustained improvements in organizational knowledge, attitudes, and policies — but that these improvements were substantially stronger when training was accompanied by structured follow-up and peer consultation (Mangus et al., 2023).

Parallel research on train-the-trainer models in gender-based violence education consistently finds that the degree of practice change is predicted less by the quality of the initial training and more by the quality of the support structure that follows it (Şimşek & Uğur, 2024). A well-designed training without follow-up produces knowledge. A well-designed training with structured follow-up produces practice.

This is why the Community of Practice session is not an optional add-on to Steady Ground. It is part of the programme's design — the component that makes the investment in Days 1 and 2 pay off in the rooms that matter.

"A well-designed training without follow-up produces knowledge. A well-designed training with structured follow-up produces practice."

Approach & Philosophy

On the intersection of theory and practice.

On trauma-informed work

Trauma-informed practice is not a collection of techniques. It is a way of being in the room — with people, with content, and with the uncertainty that is part of any genuine encounter. Frameworks like SAMHSA's six principles, Polyvagal Theory, and IFS provide the language and the structure. The relationship provides the ground.

Rachael brings this orientation to everything she does: to her clinical work, to her supervision, to her training, and to the organizations she consults with. What does it mean to be genuinely safe here? What does it mean to share power in a way that is more than performative? These questions are not answered once — they are returned to, in every room, with every group.

On feminist pedagogy

A feminist approach to education holds that the form of learning is never neutral. The arrangement of the room, the structure of the agenda, the degree to which participants' knowledge is treated as expertise — these are not administrative details. They are pedagogical choices that communicate something about whose knowledge counts and whose experience matters.

Rachael's teaching and training practice is built on the conviction that the most powerful learning happens when people feel genuinely seen, genuinely safe, and genuinely able to bring their own expertise into the room. Steady Ground is designed to produce exactly that.

On organizational health

Much of Rachael's consulting work focuses on the organizational conditions that make good clinical and educational work possible — or impossible. Burnout, moral distress, vicarious trauma, and the erosion of professional identity are not individual failures. They are organizational symptoms, and they require organizational responses.

She brings this systems lens to her training work as well. Steady Ground is not just about building individual facilitator competence — it is about building the conditions under which that competence can be sustained. The facilitator care plan, the Community of Practice, the peer consultation model — these are not extras. They are the programme's commitment to the people it is training.

Areas of practice

Rachael's clinical and consulting work spans trauma-informed care, feminist organizational practice, clinical supervision, adult learning and professional development, and vicarious trauma prevention. She is available for organizational consultation, clinical supervision, and training design on an ongoing basis.
